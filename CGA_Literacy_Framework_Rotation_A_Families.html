The Approach

One framework.
Every reader.

Every student — from kindergarten to 6th grade — works through the same big questions together. The difference is which book they're holding.

1

Everyone launches together

Each unit begins with a shared text or film clip that the whole class experiences together, building common vocabulary and a shared question before students branch into their own books.

2

Students choose their book

From a curated menu spanning 2nd through 7th grade reading levels, students pick a book in their zone — in a genre they love. The lens, not the book, holds the class together.

3

Nonfiction meets fiction mid-unit

Every unit includes a shared nonfiction text the whole class reads together — connecting the literary lens to a real content area like history, science, or biography.

4

Everyone discusses together

The Socratic seminar is where it all comes together — students arguing from different books, different evidence, toward the same big question. Mixed-age discussion is one of CGA's greatest assets.

5

Writing with evidence

Each unit ends with writing that asks students to make an argument, not just retell. Opinion and analytical writing build across the year toward the most ambitious piece in the spring.

6

Two rotations, one complete education

Rotation A focuses on nonfiction, argument, biography, and folklore. Rotation B (the alternating year) centers on fiction and literary analysis. Together they cover every major reading and writing standard.

Unit 1 — Reading Between the Lines

September – October · Paired with Geography & Community

What your child will do

Students learn to read what's underneath what an author actually says — the feelings, beliefs, and meanings that are implied rather than stated. We launch with picture books that reward inference (like Voices in the Park, where four characters experience the same scene with wildly different perspectives) before students branch into their own books. Mid-unit, the class reads nonfiction about how geography shapes communities — and asks the same question: what does this photograph or map leave out?

The big question

"What does the author want you to feel or believe that they never actually say out loud?"

Some books students might choose

Smile (graphic novel) Charlotte's Web The One and Only Ivan Bud, Not Buddy The Giver A Long Walk to Water The House on Mango Street

Film connection

We use Inside Out (emotions as subtext made visible) and Zootopia (what the film implies about bias vs. what it says directly) to build the shared vocabulary before students branch into books.

For K–2 students

Younger students explore inference through shared picture book read-alouds — The Mysteries of Harris Burdick (images that demand inference with no text to rescue you), Voices in the Park, and Home by Carson Ellis. The question is the same: what did the author leave out? K–2 students respond by drawing what the author implied rather than writing it.

For 3rd and 4th graders

Partner reading from a tiered menu — picture books for developing readers, early chapter books for mid-level, and options like The One and Only Ivan or Bud, Not Buddy for strong readers. Journal responses are written but scaffolded: "The author never said ___, but I could tell because ___."

Unit 2 — Forces at Work

October – November · Paired with Physical Science & Scientists

What your child will do

Scientists and inventors read the world the way good readers read books — they notice what doesn't fit, ask why, form a hypothesis, and test it. This unit pairs physical science content with books about curious people figuring things out. The anchor series for younger students is Zoey & Sassafrass, which makes the scientific method the plot. Older students read scientist biographies and fiction about inventors, asking: what does it take to keep going when you don't know if you're right?

The big question

"What makes someone keep going when they don't know if they're right?"

Some books students might choose

Zoey & Sassafrass (series) Secret Coders (graphic novel) The Invention of Hugo Cabret Counting by 7s Hidden Figures (Young Readers Ed.) The Wright Brothers

Film connection

Clips from October Sky (boys who teach themselves rocketry in a coal mining town) and Hidden Figures. For younger students, Honey, I Shrunk the Kids works as a lighter entry point about what happens when experiments go wrong.

For K–2 students

Zoey & Sassafrass serves as the whole-class anchor for K–2 — we read it together as a class and use it to build the vocabulary of scientific inquiry: question, observe, predict, test, revise. Picture book biographies like Rosie Revere, Engineer bring the lens to real scientists.

For 3rd and 4th graders

Partner reading includes the Zoey & Sassafrass series, the Who Was? scientist biographies (Marie Curie, Jane Goodall, Albert Einstein), and for strong readers, The Invention of Hugo Cabret or Counting by 7s.

Unit 3 — Who Has Power? Whose Voice Matters?

January – February · Paired with History & Civics

What your child will do

This is our biography unit — but with a specific lens: students read real people's lives as arguments about power, fairness, and resistance. Every biography makes choices about whose story matters and how to tell it. Students study case clusters (the American Revolution, the Civil Rights Movement, and global figures like Malala), build a "bottle biography" hands-on project for their chosen figure, and then choose a final performance task to show their understanding.

The big question

"When someone uses their voice against an unfair system, what are they risking — and what makes it worth it?"

Some books students might choose

I Am Rosa Parks Malala's Magic Pencil Who Was? series Number the Stars Chains Uprising Roll of Thunder, Hear My Cry

The bottle biography project

All students build a bottle biography — a hands-on model representing the life of a historical figure, using symbols, images, and labels to show important events, moments of unfairness, and acts of resistance. This is built throughout the unit, not just at the end, and serves as the foundation for the final performance task.

For K–2 students

Shared picture book biographies anchor K–2 — Rosa by Nikki Giovanni, Martin's Big Words, Brave Girl (Clara Lemlich). Students discuss: who had power? Was it fair? What did they do? Bottle biographies for younger students rely on drawing and oral explanation.

For 3rd and 4th graders

Partner reading includes the Who Was? series, Ruth and the Green Book, and for strong readers, Number the Stars or I Am Malala (Young Readers Edition). Final task options include an artifact box, a letter from the person's perspective, or a concept map.

Unit 4 — Survival & the Natural World

February – March · Paired with Animals & Ecosystems

What your child will do

This is the unit students talk about for years. Anchored by the Who Would Win series, students learn to read nonfiction like an argument — gathering evidence, weighing competing claims, and defending a position. The literary lens is comparison and argument: which animal has the better adaptation? Which would survive this specific environment? Students read across nonfiction, build the scientific knowledge to make their arguments real, and then defend them in a formal mid-unit debate.

The big question

"When two animals compete for survival, what actually determines who wins — strength, adaptations, or something else entirely?"

Some books students might choose

Who Would Win? (series) Owls in the Family The One and Only Ivan My Side of the Mountain Never Cry Wolf The Wild Robot Call of the Wild

The mid-unit debate

Students choose two animals, build an evidence-based argument for which would survive a specific scenario, and present their case to the class. The class evaluates the evidence — not just who argued most confidently, but who used the best support. This is one of the most genuinely fun academic moments of the year.

For K–2 students

Who Would Win books are the primary shared texts for K–2, read aloud as a class. Students discuss: What are the two animals? What's each one's best advantage? Which do you think would win — and why? The illustrated comparison is the K–2 culminating project.

For 3rd and 4th graders

Partner reading includes Who Would Win titles chosen by interest, Charlotte's Web, Owls in the Family, and for strong readers, Hatchet or My Side of the Mountain. Argument paragraph is the culminating writing: one claim, two pieces of evidence, one acknowledgment of the counterargument.

Unit 5 — Folklore, Fables & Myths

April – May · Paired with Earth Science

What your child will do

Every culture has stories that explain the world, teach lessons, and pass down values. Many of the oldest are about natural phenomena — thunder, seasons, why the sun moves across the sky. In December, students got a taste of this comparing Gingerbread Man variants across cultures. Now they go deep: reading myths and folklore from multiple traditions, comparing what stays the same and what changes, and asking the biggest question: what does the story a culture keeps retelling tell us about what that culture believes?

The big question

"What does the story a culture chooses to keep and retell tell us about what that culture believes?"

Some books students might choose

D'Aulaires' Book of Greek Myths Why Mosquitoes Buzz in People's Ears Arlo Finch (graphic novel) The Lightning Thief Anansi Boys The Epic of Gilgamesh

The Earth Science connection

Many myths explain natural phenomena — thunder, earthquakes, seasons. Students compare what a myth says about thunder to what the science says, asking: what does each explanation do that the other can't? What does the myth give us that the science can't? This is one of the richest cross-curricular discussions of the year.

For K–2 students

Shared picture book folklore from multiple cultures — Why Mosquitoes Buzz in People's Ears, Anansi stories, pourquoi tales. K–2 students write and illustrate their own pourquoi tale ("Why the _____ is _____"). The question is accessible at every age: why do you think people made up this story?

For 3rd and 4th graders

Partner reading from folklore collections and myth retellings. Culminating writing is a written fable with a stated moral, or a paragraph arguing what two myths from different cultures have in common.

Questions

What families ask us

A few things that come up often.

How is Rotation A different from Rotation B?
Rotation B (the alternating year) focuses on fiction and literary analysis — hero's journey, character transformation, point of view, and belonging. Rotation A pulls harder on nonfiction, argument, biography, and real-world texts. A student who completes both has read across every major genre and practiced every major reading and writing standard. The rotations are designed as two halves of a complete education in literary thinking.
How does my child choose their book? What if they pick something too easy or hard?
We guide students toward books that are a great fit for their reading level — but we frame it around genre and interest, not labels. A student who loves animals might choose Who Would Win, Owls in the Family, or The Wild Robot depending on where they are as a reader. We'll talk with you and your child at the start of each unit if there are any concerns.
Will my child read the same books as their classmates?
Not necessarily — and that's the point. Students choose from a menu of 8–12 books per unit, so a mixed-age group might have five or six different books in one class discussion. The magic is that they're all answering the same question, just with different evidence. This develops higher-order thinking skills that reading the same book together simply can't.
What is the bottle biography project?
In Unit 3, every student builds a bottle biography — a hands-on model of a historical figure's life using symbols, images, and labels. The bottle represents the person: who they were, what they faced, and how they responded. Younger students rely on drawing and oral explanation; older students include written captions and clearer symbolism. It's built throughout the unit rather than saved for the end, so it becomes a tool for thinking rather than just a finished product.
What is a Socratic seminar and how does my child prepare for it?
A Socratic seminar is a structured discussion where students bring their books and ideas and talk through a big question together. There's no right answer — the goal is to make an argument, listen to others, and change your thinking when someone makes a good point. Students prepare by keeping a reading journal throughout the unit, so they have notes and thinking ready to bring to the discussion.
My child doesn't love reading. Will this work for them?
Student choice is one of the most powerful tools for building reading engagement. When a child picks a book they actually want to read — in a genre they care about, at a level that feels manageable — something shifts. We also include graphic novels in every unit specifically because they are fully rigorous works of literature and a great bridge for students who find prose challenging. The Who Would Win unit in particular tends to hook reluctant readers who love facts and arguments. We'd love to talk about your child specifically.
What standards does this cover?
Rotation A is built on Common Core State Standards for ELA, with a particular focus on the nonfiction and argument standards that Rotation B touches more lightly: RI.5.6 / RI.6.6 (author's purpose in informational text), RI.5.8 / RI.6.8 (evaluating arguments and claims), and the comparison standards RI.5.9 / RI.6.9. Opinion writing (W.5.1, W.6.1) builds across the year, and Unit 5 adds narrative writing (W.5.3) through the original fable or myth project.
