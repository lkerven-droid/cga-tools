Our Approach

Reading that connects

This year, our literacy program is built around four big questions — one per unit — that every student explores through the book of their choice. Here's how each unit works.

1

We launch together

Every unit starts with the whole class exploring the big idea together — through a film, a short story, or a poem — so everyone has shared vocabulary before branching out.

2

Your child chooses their book

Students choose from a curated menu of books — fiction, graphic novels, and classics — matched to their reading level and interests. Nobody reads the same book they read last year.

3

We connect to real life

Mid-unit, the whole class reads a nonfiction text that connects what they're reading to real science, history, or current events. Stories become more meaningful when they connect to the real world.

4

We discuss together

Students bring their different books to a shared discussion and compare. A student reading a dragon fantasy and a student reading a classic myth are both answering the same big question.

5

They write what they think

Each unit ends with a piece of writing where students make an argument about their book using evidence. By the end of the year, they've written across four different genres and questions.

Unit 1 — Hero's Journey

August 11 – October 3 · Paired with Dinosaurs & Rocks

What your child will do

Students learn the hero's journey framework — the ancient story structure found in everything from Greek mythology to Wings of Fire — then choose a book and track how their hero moves through it. Mid-unit, the whole class reads about a real scientist (like Mary Anning, who made her first major paleontology discovery at age 12) and compares her real-life journey to the fictional one they're reading.

The big question

"What does a hero — real or fictional — have to lose before they can truly transform?"

Some books students might choose

How to Train Your Dragon Hatchet Amulet (graphic novel) Dragon Rider Wings of Fire: Legends — Dragonslayer A Wrinkle in Time The Odyssey Esperanza Rising

Film connection

We'll use clips from Moana, The Lion King, and Hercules to build the shared framework before students branch into their books.

For K–2 students

Younger students explore the hero's journey through picture books read aloud together — Last Stop on Market Street, Brave Irene, and The Adventures of Beekle (a Caldecott winner where every stage of the hero's journey is clearly visible). They discuss the same questions as older students, just through a shared text rather than individual books.

For 3rd and 4th graders

Partner reading from a tiered menu — picture books for developing readers, early chapter books like My Father's Dragon for mid-level readers, and options like The Wild Robot or How to Train Your Dragon for strong readers.

Unit 2 — Character & Transformation

October 6 – November 21 · Paired with Native Peoples

What your child will do

Students explore the difference between external pressure and internal change — things that happen to a character versus what shifts inside them. This connects deeply to our Native Peoples unit, where we read nonfiction about Indigenous resilience and what it means to hold onto who you are when outside forces push you to change.

The big question

"Can a character change without wanting to — and is that still transformation?"

Some books students might choose

Wonder Holes El Deafo (graphic novel) Because of Winn-Dixie Inside Out and Back Again The Secret Garden Little Women

Film connection

We'll use clips from Up (the first 10 minutes are one of the most powerful character transformation sequences ever filmed), Encanto, and Brave.

For K–2 students

K–2 students explore transformation through picture books like The Name Jar (a girl who reclaims her identity rather than changing it to fit in) and Each Kindness (whose transformation is incomplete — powerful for discussing what it means when someone misses their chance to change). These conversations go surprisingly deep even with young children.

For 3rd and 4th graders

Partner reading options include Charlotte's Web, Because of Winn-Dixie, Shiloh, and for strong readers, Wonder or Holes.

Unit 3 — Point of View & Narrator's Lens

January 5 – February 20 · Paired with Poetry

What your child will do

We start this unit with poetry — because poems are the perfect place to feel how a narrator's perspective shapes every word. Students study speakers in poems by Langston Hughes, Mary Oliver, and others before choosing a novel and asking the same questions: What does this narrator know? What don't they know? Whose story isn't being told?

The big question

"Whose story isn't being told — in your novel or in the poems we read — and why does that matter?"

Some books students might choose

Coraline Counting by 7s Hilo (graphic novel) My Father's Dragon Charlotte Doyle Treasure Island Fever 1793

Film connection

Harriet the Spy (a notebook is literally a narrator's lens), Megamind (what it looks like when the villain tells the story), and The Princess Bride.

For K–2 students

The True Story of the Three Little Pigs is the anchor at every grade level — we use it first, always. K–2 students also explore Voices in the Park (four characters experience the same park visit with wildly different perspectives) and Duck! Rabbit! as a playful entry point. These are picture books doing genuinely sophisticated literary work.

For 3rd and 4th graders

Partner reading options include Nate the Great (a first-person narrator detective — what does he miss?), Hilo as a graphic novel option, and Coraline or The View from Saturday for strong readers.

Unit 4 — Belonging & Identity

February 23 – April 17 · Paired with Plants & Bugs

What your child will do

Our capstone unit pairs beautifully with Plants & Bugs — because metamorphosis is one of the oldest metaphors for becoming who you were meant to be. Students read about caterpillars that have to completely dissolve before they can reform, and connect that to their novels. The big seminar question this unit tends to produce the most powerful discussions of the year.

The big question

"Does belonging require you to change who you are, or does it require finding people who accept who you already are?"

Some books students might choose

Front Desk The Giver New Kid (graphic novel) The Girl Who Drank the Moon The Outsiders Refugee Anne of Green Gables

Film connection

Coco is our anchor film — it holds belonging to family, culture, and your own dreams all in tension at once. We also use Lilo and Stitch and Soul.

For K–2 students

The Velveteen Rabbit is our K–2 anchor — the question of becoming "real" through love maps almost perfectly onto our seminar question about belonging. We also use Big Al (a fish who keeps trying to change himself to fit in, until he discovers the answer was never changing at all) and other picture books about finding your place. The conversations kindergarteners have with these books are extraordinary.

For 3rd and 4th graders

Partner reading includes Charlotte's Web, Because of Winn-Dixie, and for strong readers, Front Desk, The Girl Who Drank the Moon, or New Kid as a graphic novel.

Questions

What families ask us

A few things that come up often.

How does my child choose their book? What if they pick something too easy or hard?
We guide students toward books that are a great fit for their reading level — but we frame it around genre and interest, not labels. A student who loves dragons might choose How to Train Your Dragon or The Hobbit depending on where they are as a reader. We'll talk with you and your child at the start of each unit if there are any concerns.
Will my child read the same books as their classmates?
Not necessarily — and that's the point. Students choose from a menu of 8–12 books per unit, so a mixed-age group might have five or six different books in one class discussion. The magic is that they're all answering the same question, just with different evidence. This develops higher-order thinking skills that reading the same book together can't.
How does the nonfiction pairing work?
Mid-way through each unit, the whole class reads and discusses one nonfiction text together. This might be a picture book biography, a chapter from a longer book, or a short article — depending on the age range. The goal is to connect what students are reading in their novels to something real. During the hero's journey unit, we read about Mary Anning, who made her first major scientific discovery at 12 years old. Students then compare her real journey to the fictional one they're tracking in their novel.
What is a Socratic seminar and how does my child prepare for it?
A Socratic seminar is a structured discussion where students bring their books and ideas and talk through a big question together. There's no right answer — the goal is to make an argument, listen to others, and change your thinking when someone makes a good point. Students prepare by keeping a reading journal throughout the unit, so they have notes and thinking ready to bring to the discussion.
My child doesn't love reading. Will this work for them?
Student choice is one of the most powerful tools for building reading engagement. When a child picks a book they actually want to read — in a genre they care about, at a level that feels manageable — something shifts. We also include graphic novels in every unit specifically because they are fully rigorous works of literature and they're a great bridge for students who find prose challenging or unexciting. We'd love to talk about your child specifically and help find the right fit.
What standards does this cover?
The framework is built on Common Core State Standards for ELA, specifically the reading literature standards (RL.5 and RL.6) for story structure, character, point of view, and cross-text comparison — as well as informational reading standards (RI.5 and RI.6) and opinion writing standards (W.5.1 and W.6.1). The capstone essay in Unit 4 is the most rigorous writing piece of the year and directly addresses grade-level writing standards.

Questions about your reader?

We'd love to talk through how this framework fits your child and what books might be the best fit for them.
